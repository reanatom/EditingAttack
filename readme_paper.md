# Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) often memorize sensitive information, necessitating mechanisms for "unlearning" or "model editing" to comply with privacy regulations. Existing Locate-then-Edit algorithms (e.g., ROME, MEMIT, AlphaEdit) offer efficient solutions by directly modifying model parameters. In this work, we uncover a critical vulnerability in this paradigm, termed the "Privacy Paradox": the very process of editing to protect privacy creates a side-channel that exposes the targeted sensitive information. We propose a novel two-stage inference attack that exploits the low-rank structure of algebraic parameter updates. First, we theoretically demonstrate that the row space of the update matrix encodes a "fingerprint" of the edited subjects, enabling precise Subject Inference via spectral analysis. Second, we introduce an entropy-based Prompt Recovery attack that identifies the semantic context of the edit. Extensive experiments across GPT-2 XL, GPT-J, and Llama-3 show that our attacks achieve near-perfect recovery of scrubbed data. To mitigate this, we propose "Subspace Camouflage," a defense strategy that obfuscates the update fingerprint with semantic decoys, effectively thwarting inversion attacks without compromising editing utility.

# Conclusion

This paper highlights a fundamental tension between model editability and privacy preservation. We demonstrated that state-of-the-art model editing algorithms, while effective for knowledge correction, inadvertently leak the very information they are tasked with erasing. By rigorously analyzing the linear algebraic structure of parameter updates, we developed a robust attack framework capable of recovering both the subjects and semantic contexts of privacy-preserving edits. Our findings suggest that current editing techniques are insufficient for secure machine unlearning in white-box settings. However, our proposed defense, Subspace Camouflage, offers a promising direction by masking the algebraic signatures of edits. Future work must further explore the theoretical bounds of privacy in parameter-efficient editing and develop more resilient unlearning protocols that decouple the update mechanism from the sensitive data manifold.
